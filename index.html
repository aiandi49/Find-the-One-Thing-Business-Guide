1Find your bottleneck

Before you fix anything, find the one thing actually stopping growth.

Most businesses have a dozen things that feel broken at once — the website, the follow-up, the pricing, the team. But usually only one of them is the real ceiling. Everything else is a symptom of that one constraint, and fixing the symptoms first just wastes effort.

A bottleneck is whatever the business can’t get past no matter what else improves. A limiting belief is a close cousin: an assumption that’s been treated as fact for so long that nobody’s tested it — “our customers would never accept that,” “only I can do this part.” Those beliefs quietly set a ceiling long before any real constraint does.

CASE SNAPSHOT — A FAMILY DENTAL PRACTICE

One dentist did every high-value procedure himself, booked a month out, and couldn’t justify hiring a second dentist without proof there’d be enough work to fill their schedule. The assumption was that patients would only trust the owner. Once the practice mapped a single treatment into its individual visits and asked, honestly, which steps truly needed the owner, most of them didn’t. That one exercise — not a new ad campaign — was what actually unlocked capacity.

2Know your numbers

Revenue is just three numbers multiplied together.

Leads times close rate times price. Most owners jump straight to “we need more leads” when the honest answer is usually one of the other two. If close rate is weak, more leads just means more people leaking out of a broken process, faster.

A useful rule: fix conversion before you pay for more traffic. And don’t assume raising price loses money — a smaller number of buyers at a meaningfully higher price is very often a straightforward win, as the calculator here shows.

CASE SNAPSHOT, CONTINUED

Once the practice had spare capacity, it made sense to spend more on advertising. But price moved up first — the team found that a modest drop in close rate at a much higher price still meant more revenue per booked visit, and a lot less pressure on the calendar.

3Build the offer

Make the offer worth the price before you try to sell it harder.

An offer feels expensive when the price is unclear relative to the outcome. It feels obvious when the outcome is big, the path to it feels believable, it doesn’t take forever, and it doesn’t ask for much sacrifice along the way. Improve any of those four and you can usually charge more without pushing harder.

None of that works, though, if you’re describing it to everyone. The sharper you can get about exactly who it’s for, the easier every later step becomes — the marketing, the sales conversation, even the price.

CASE SNAPSHOT, CONTINUED

“Dental patients” is not a customer. “An adult missing one or more teeth who’s been putting off treatment because of cost or fear” is. Once the practice got that specific, the guarantees, photos, and language used in every appointment could speak directly to that one person’s actual hesitation — instead of trying to reassure everyone a little.

Definition

High ticket

An offer priced high enough that each individual sale matters a lot — fewer sales, bigger dollars, and every conversation counts.

Definition

Avatar

The one specific type of customer you’re building the offer, the marketing, and the sales pitch for.

Definition

What makes an offer feel worth it

A big believable outcome, delivered quickly, without asking too much of the customer along the way.

4Map the journey

Trace every step from stranger to customer

Draw the actual sequence your business uses today, then look for friction at each step — not what you assume happens, what actually happens.

1

Someone raises a hand

A lead: someone who clicked, called, or filled out a form. What matters here is simply how many arrive per week.

2

They get an answer, fast

Speed to first contact is one of the biggest silent leaks in most businesses. A great lead contacted two days late is often a lost one.

3

They arrive already informed

A short video or page that answers the obvious questions before the real conversation starts, so the meeting itself can focus on their specific situation.

4

The actual conversation

Your sales motion: the exact sequence from “interested” to “paid.” Walk it yourself, looking for the point where people usually hesitate.

5

A clear next step

A call to action: the explicit instruction for what to do next. Content without one entertains. Content with one moves someone forward.

Common objections, and a starting response

“I need to think about the cost”+
Usually a signal that value hasn’t been made obvious yet, not that the price is genuinely wrong. Revisit the outcome before you revisit the number.
“Not the right time”+
Ask what would need to be true for the timing to work. Often there’s a real, fixable reason underneath — and sometimes a payment plan is the actual answer.
“I want to get another opinion”+
A trust gap. Proof — real photos, numbers, or stories from people like them — usually closes this faster than more persuasion.
“I need to check with my partner”+
Fair, and worth respecting. Give them something concrete to bring back to that conversation, rather than leaving it to memory.
Definition

Financing

A third party pays you in full today; the customer repays that lender over time. You get paid now, they get a payment plan.

Definition

Downsell

A smaller, easier option offered only if someone turns down the main offer — a fallback, never the default.

Definition

Before & after proof

Concrete evidence of the transformation you deliver — photos, numbers, or stories that do the convincing for you.

5Remove the friction

Good sales quietly die from small, fixable friction.

Most lost sales aren’t about the price itself — they’re about the gap between what something costs today and what the customer can pay today. Financing and payment plans close that gap without you discounting anything.

Proof works the same way for trust. Nobody has to take your word for the outcome if you can just show it to them — clearly, specifically, and from someone who looks like them.

CASE SNAPSHOT, CONTINUED

Adding a financing option didn’t change the treatment price at all — it changed whether patients could say yes today instead of putting it off indefinitely. The pay-per-visit option stayed on the table, but only as a fallback, never presented first.

6Grow without breaking

What tends to snap first as you grow

Every stage of growth trades one set of problems for another. Knowing what’s coming next means you can build the fix before the constraint actually bites.

Just you

Everything runs through one person

What usually breaks

You are the bottleneck. Quality is consistent because you touch everything, but so is your ceiling.

What helps

Write down how you actually do the work, before you need to hand any of it off.

First few hires

You’re delegating, but loosely

What usually breaks

Inconsistent quality, unclear ownership, and no simple way to see what’s actually working.

What helps

A short handbook, a shared place to track leads and customers, and a handful of numbers you check weekly.

Small team

Systems matter more than effort

What usually breaks

Word-of-mouth processes stop scaling. What worked informally starts producing real inconsistency.

What helps

Turn your best person’s habits into a repeatable process everyone else can follow, not just imitate.

Revisit this diagnostic quarterly

Your bottleneck moves as you grow. What was true six months ago probably isn’t now.

Fix one thing at a time

Changing price, marketing, and the sales process all at once makes it impossible to know what worked.
